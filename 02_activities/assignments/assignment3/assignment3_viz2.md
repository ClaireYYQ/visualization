Original data link:https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/motor-vehicle-collisions-involving-killed-or-seriously-injured-persons/

> What software did you use to create your data visualization?
I used Excel.

> Who is your intended audience? 
The intended audience is the general public.

> What information or message are you trying to convey with your visualization? 
The visualization aims to show how the number of motor vehicle collisions involving at least one person who was killed or seriously injured changes over time. It highlights trends across years to help viewers understand whether road safety is improving or worsening.

> What aspects of design did you consider when making your visualization? How did you apply them? With what elements of your plots? 
Findability: I included a clear and descriptive title indicating that the chart shows trends in motor vehicle collisions over time. The x-axis and y-axis are explicitly labeled, making it easy for viewers to quickly identify what the data represents.
Accessibility: I used a simple line chart to present trends over time, as it is easy to interpret by a general audience. The color scheme was kept minimal with sufficient contrast to ensure readability. Font sizes and labels were chosen to be clear and legible, and unnecessary visual clutter was avoided.
Interoperability: The visualization uses standard formats, such as common year format, making it easy to interpret across different platforms and by users with varying backgrounds.
Reusability: The data processing steps were kept straightforward and documented, enabling others to reproduce or adapt the visualization for future analyses.
    
> How did you ensure that your data visualizations are reproducible? If the tool you used to make your data visualization is not reproducible, how will this impact your data visualization? 
Reproducibility was supported by documenting each step of the data processing workflow. It includes the extraction of the year information and the count of unique collision IDs per year using UNIQUE and FILTER. The original dataset structure was also preserved, allowing others to replicate the same results using the same inputs.

> How did you ensure that your data visualization is accessible?  
Accessibility was ensured by using clear axis labels, simple line chart format, readable text, high contrast, and highlighted data values.

> Who are the individuals and communities who might be impacted by your visualization?
Broader society benefits from improved understanding of traffic risk trends and prevention strategies. It is also relevant to public policy professionals who can use collision data to guide safety interventions. 
   
> How did you choose which features of your chosen dataset to include or exclude from your visualization? 
I selected only 6 years and the related number of motor vehicle collisions because the goal of the visualization was to show how serious collisions change over time. This simplification helped highlight long-term patterns without introducing unnecessary complexity.

> What ‘underwater labour’ contributed to your final data visualization product?
‘Underwater labour’ included cleaning and filtering the dataset to include only six years of data, counting unique collision events per year, formatting the chart, and adjusting labels and axes for clarity.
